From Formulation to Articulation:
Considerations for AI-Assisted Research in Information Theory

The paper examines documented cases of AI assistance and introduces a framework for describing the research process. It is the starting collection for this project. We invite new cases, corrections, and updates to the evidence as this area develops.

Our focus is the mathematical contribution, the roles of researchers and AI systems, the checks supporting the claims, and the effort to make the ideas understandable.

The classification framework

Four stages.
Five separate classifications.

Each code answers a different question about the process. The codes do not combine into a single score of research quality, autonomy, or intellectual credit.

01 · FFormulation02 · SAI-mediated solution03 · A + NVerification04 · TArticulation
F

Problem formulation

Who identified and materially shaped the consequential research question?

F-0 Human-formulated
A researcher supplies a sufficiently specific theorem, conjecture, construction, or counterexample problem.
F-1 AI-refined
AI proposes a consequential subproblem, reduction, conjecture, or reformulation that materially changes a human-supplied direction.
F-2 AI-originated
Under broad domain guidance, AI identifies the question and gives a substantive account of its novelty and significance before it is selected.
S

AI-mediated solution

How did researchers interact with the AI system while developing the result?

S-0 Direct prompting
The reported process consists of a prompt and a candidate idea, construction, or proof, possibly with brief follow-up exchanges.
S-1 Interactive collaboration
Researchers and AI repeatedly exchange ideas, objections, computations, and revised arguments, with human feedback guiding development.
S-2 Harnessed search or agentic workflow
AI operates within a structured process involving execution, evaluators, search, external tools, multiple agents, or automated iteration.

Insufficient disclosure: use ND when the evidence does not support a specific classification. Naming a model does not establish its interaction mode; incomplete disclosure alone does not justify S-0.

Two kinds of evidence

Verification needs both sides of the argument.

A numerical certificate establishes a finite obligation. The analytical reasoning connecting that obligation to the theorem requires its own verification.

A

Analytical reasoning

A-0 No documented complete check
The public record does not document a human expert or proof assistant checking every indispensable analytical step.
A-1 Author verification
The authors check or reconstruct the complete reasoning chain. Responsibility for a manuscript alone is not evidence of a complete check.
A-2 Independent expert verification
A qualified expert outside the proof-generation process checks the particular argument and its indispensable reductions.
A-3 Formal verification
The analytical argument is formalized and checked in Lean or another proof assistant, relative to a stated trusted base.
N

Numerical certification

N-0 No numerical proof obligation
The proof is analytical, or numerical work is exploratory and carries no logical burden for the result.
N-1 Ordinary numerical evidence
Floating-point experiments, Monte Carlo results, or plots support a claim without a rigorous enclosure.
N-2 Rigorous finite certificate
Exact arithmetic, rigorous interval or ball arithmetic, an exhaustive finite check, or a checkable witness establishes a specified finite obligation.
N-3 Independent replay or formal integration
The certificate is reproduced independently, or its checker and connection to the theorem are formally verified.

Overall verification follows the indispensable steps. An A-0/N-2 claim remains provisional when the analytical reduction is unchecked. An independent proof corroborates a theorem statement without verifying another derivation. Formalization also requires checking that the formal statement faithfully represents the theorem in the paper.

T

Translation and articulation

How were the ideas and results developed into an account for human understanding?

T-0 Machine-led presentation
The argument or exposition is substantially AI-generated, without documented substantive human reconstruction beyond direction, feedback, or final review.
T-1 Author-revised AI exposition
Authors substantively review, edit, reorganize, and clarify AI-originated material. Human-directed AI rewriting also belongs here when authors guide and review the resulting exposition.
T-2 Human-led articulation
Researchers construct or substantially reorganize the exposition around the ideas, techniques, interpretations, significance, and limitations.

Classify the relevant component

A key lemma, a construction, and the rest of a proof may have different histories. Describe distinct components when possible; use a paper-level account when the collaboration is too intertwined to separate meaningfully.

Explain the character of the work

Distinguish conceptual reasoning from search and optimization. A human/AI percentage is not required. Any optional reasoning/computation estimate should state what it measures and its uncertainty.

Keep origin, checking, and explanation distinct

Human verification does not change who developed an argument. Clear exposition does not establish correctness, and formal correctness does not by itself provide an explanation of the ideas.

How to interpret entries with different verification levels

For the multibit extension, N-2 refers to finite construction checks and N-1 to evidence motivating the small-output conjecture. For deletion-correcting codes, the one-deletion theorem is analytical (N-0), while finite checks support other constructions (N-2). The Fisher-information paper’s supplementary grid calculation carries no proof obligation (N-0).

A-0 means that a complete analytical check is not documented; it does not establish that no check occurred. The T classification concerns articulation and does not by itself establish analytical verification. See the paper’s comparison and discussion for the basis and scope of these assessments.

Help extend the collection

Bring a case.
Show the evidence.

We welcome published papers, preprints, repositories, and public author accounts documenting substantive AI assistance in information-theoretic research.

  1. Check for an existing discussion.

    Search the repository’s issues for the paper title, authors, or arXiv identifier. Add new evidence to an existing issue when appropriate.

  2. Open “Suggest a research case.”

    Sign in to GitHub and use the submission button below, or choose Issues → New issue in the repository. No code changes are needed.

  3. Describe the result and AI contribution.

    Provide the full reference, public source links, and the specific theorem, lemma, construction, or other component. Point to the AI-use disclosure.

  4. Report the verification evidence.

    Explain who checked the analytical steps and what the numerical checks establish. Suggest classifications if the evidence supports them; “not documented” is a useful answer.

  5. Discuss and refine.

    Maintainers review the sources and may request clarification before incorporating a case into the collection or a future revision. An issue submission is not an endorsement of correctness.

What to include

  • Title, authors, date, and a public reference
  • The contribution and why it matters
  • Evidence of AI assistance, including models and workflow when disclosed
  • Analytical verification and numerical certification, reported separately
  • How the result was articulated for human readers
  • Relevant limitations or missing information

Classification suggestions are welcome but optional. You do not need to reproduce the proof to propose a case.

Have a correction or update?

Use the correction form for new verification evidence, revised papers, changed classifications, or factual errors.
